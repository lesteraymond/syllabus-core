How to use this booklet

LCUP is evaluating an AI-assisted syllabus checker. Before any comparison, the checker needs a faculty reference: independent judgments by qualified faculty. You are asked to judge 21 academic relationships drawn from three actual LCUP syllabi. You will not see, and should not look for, any output of the checker or of any other AI tool.

  1. Read the rubric that follows. It is provisional, and Part 6 lists the points that LCUP has not yet defined.
  2. Your form lists your cases. For each one, read its two statements and its context here: the course description, the CLOs, the lesson outline and the whole week as printed.
  3. Record your judgment in the evaluation form (Excel), one row per case, in the yellow cells: the judgment, its justification, any recommended correction and your reviewer ID.
  4. Work alone, and return the form to the research coordinator.

These materials reproduce parts of approved LCUP syllabi for this review only. Keep them on a university computer. Do not upload them to any cloud service or AI tool, and return or delete them when the review ends.

Statements are reproduced from the approved syllabi as transcribed for the project. Line breaks of the printed table were joined, and bullets are shown one per line; spelling, numbering and CLO tags are unchanged, including evident typing errors.

The rubric (provisional)

Provisional draft, prepared on 2026-10-29 for the capstone research team and the faculty reviewers. It is not an LCUP policy, and it supplies no official LCUP definition. The faculty panel should review it, and the office the institution designates should approve or amend it, before it is treated as an institutional standard. It builds on the project's existing reviewer guide and on the seven academic relationships of the evaluation policy (academic-evaluation-v2-preview-1), and changes neither.

Part 1. What you are judging

One academic relationship at a time, between two statements of one syllabus, using only the evidence shown with the case. You are not judging the whole syllabus, its format, its approval or its author. The syllabi were approved for use; that approval does not establish that every relationship in them is aligned.

Part 2. The four judgments

ALIGNEDThe supplied evidence supports this particular academic relationship.
PARTIALLY_ALIGNEDThere is meaningful support, but a material gap or ambiguity remains.
NOT_ALIGNEDThere is a substantiated mismatch in this particular relationship.
INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCEThe supplied information cannot support a defensible judgment.

How to choose

  1. Read the two statements in full, then the context: the whole week as printed, the course description and the CLOs.
  2. Note what each statement is about (its subject matter) and, where it says so, what students are to do.
  3. Ask the question of the relationship (Part 4) about this pair only.
  4. Choose ALIGNED if the evidence supports the relationship and you see no material gap; PARTIALLY_ALIGNED if there is meaningful support but you can name a material gap or ambiguity; NOT_ALIGNED if you can point to the mismatch in the text; INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE if the statements, as written, cannot support a defensible judgment either way.
  5. A gap is material if, in your professional judgment, it would change what is taught, practised or assessed. Say which gap you mean.
  6. Write your justification and, if needed, a recommended correction (Part 5).

Part 3. Academic alignment and structural compliance

This review concerns academic alignment: whether the meaning of two statements fits together. It does not concern structural compliance: whether the syllabus has the required parts in the required form. Structural compliance is checked separately, by rule-based checks that this review does not change.

Structural compliance: not judged hereAcademic alignment: judged here
Whether a field is present: an ILO carries a CLO tag, a lesson week has an ILO, a lesson names a core valueWhether the content of the two statements fits together
Format and numbering: codes, labels, week numbers, tags written in different stylesWhether what one statement requires is supported by the other
Counts and totals: number of CLOs, references, grading weightsWhether the scope and level of the two statements are consistent
Approval and sign-off of the document

A structural problem does not by itself make a relationship misaligned, and a well-formed statement can still be misaligned. If you notice a structural problem, record it under Notes and base your judgment on meaning.

Part 4. The seven relationships

4.1 Course Title ↔ Course Content

Question. Does this week's course content fall within the subject that the course title names, read together with the course description?

Consider

  • the subject the title names, and the scope and level the course description gives it.
  • every topic the week lists, not only its first line.
  • the lesson outline, which shows where the week sits in the course.
ALIGNEDThe week's topics belong to the subject, scope and level that the title and description indicate.
PARTIALLY_ALIGNEDSome topics belong; others fall outside that scope or level, or the connection is indirect.
NOT_ALIGNEDThe week's topics belong to a different subject.
INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCEThe content, as written, cannot be compared with the title.

Needs an institutional definition

  • How an orientation week (course requirements, objectives, grading and policies) should be judged against the course title is not defined by LCUP. Choose the judgment you can defend and explain your reading. Answer "yes" to "Requires an institutional decision?" if your judgment depends on it.

4.2 Course Content ↔ ILO

Question. Does this week's course content provide the subject matter that the ILO expects students to learn or demonstrate?

Consider

  • what the ILO names, against the topics the week lists.
  • the ILO's scope: if it names topics taught in other weeks, whether this week still supplies a meaningful part of it.
  • whether the content lets students do what the ILO's verb asks (for example, apply, design or analyse what is listed).
ALIGNEDThe content covers what the ILO names.
PARTIALLY_ALIGNEDThe content covers part of what the ILO names, or the ILO reaches beyond this content in a way that matters.
NOT_ALIGNEDThe ILO names subject matter that this content does not include.
INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCEThe content or the ILO is too general or too incomplete to compare.

Needs an institutional definition

  • Some ILOs concern values formation (for example, "Demonstrate the Filipino-Catholic Augustinian Spirituality through value webbing") and appear in lessons on technical topics. How LCUP expects such an ILO to relate to the week's technical content is not defined. Explain your reading. Answer "yes" to "Requires an institutional decision?" if your judgment depends on it.

4.3 ILO ↔ CLO

Question. Does achieving this ILO contribute to the CLO it is mapped to?

Consider

  • whether the ILO develops part of what the CLO states (its competency and subject). An ILO need not cover the whole CLO; several ILOs usually build one CLO.
  • the CLO's required LCUP classification, which follows from its number: CLO 1 Introductory, CLO 2 Enabling, CLO 3 Demonstrating, CLO 4 Integration of Values. Consider whether the ILO's purpose and level suit that classification as you understand it. The classification belongs to the CLO, not to the week.
  • each mapping on its own: an ILO mapped to two CLOs forms two relationships, and each is judged separately.
  • the week's content, which shows what the ILO concerns in that lesson.
ALIGNEDThe ILO develops a recognisable part of the CLO, in a way consistent with the CLO's classification as you understand it.
PARTIALLY_ALIGNEDThe ILO develops part of the CLO, but an element that matters (for example the CLO's distinctive competency, or the fit with its classification) is missing or doubtful.
NOT_ALIGNEDThe ILO develops nothing that the CLO states.
INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCEThe statements are too general or too incomplete to decide.

Needs an institutional definition

  • The official definitions of the four classifications are not available to this project, and this rubric does not supply them. Do not assume one. If your judgment depends on what a classification means, write the meaning you applied in your justification. Answer "yes" to "Requires an institutional decision?" if your judgment depends on it.
  • Whether technical ILOs may also be mapped to the values CLO (CLO 4, Integration of Values) is not defined.
  • ILOs that are mapped to no CLO are listed separately by the research team and are not part of this review.

4.4 Course Content ↔ Learning Activities

Question. Do this week's learning activities engage students with the week's course content?

Consider

  • whether the activities name, or clearly address, the topics the week lists.
  • coverage: whether the activities reach the main topics or only some of them.
ALIGNEDThe activities address the content's topics.
PARTIALLY_ALIGNEDThe activities address some of the topics, or address them only indirectly.
NOT_ALIGNEDThe activities address a different subject.
INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCEThe activities, as written, cannot be compared with the content.

Needs an institutional definition

  • LCUP does not say whether an activity or assessment worded only in general terms (for example, a teaching method, or a quiz "on the topics discussed") is enough evidence. It can be read as saying too little to judge, or read together with the week's content. Choose the reading you can defend, say which one you used, and answer "yes" to "Requires an institutional decision?".

4.5 ILO ↔ Learning Activities

Question. Do the learning activities give students the opportunity to do what the ILO states?

Consider

  • the ILO's verb (what students must be able to do, for example explain, apply or design) and its object.
  • whether at least one activity lets students practise that verb on that object.
  • whether the kind of activity suits the kind of outcome, for example whether students practise a skill the ILO names.
ALIGNEDAt least one activity lets students do what the ILO states.
PARTIALLY_ALIGNEDThe activities address the ILO's subject but not what students must do, or only part of it.
NOT_ALIGNEDNo activity addresses the ILO's subject or what it asks students to do.
INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCEThe statements are too general to compare.

Needs an institutional definition

  • What activity evidence LCUP expects for a values-formation ILO (value webbing) is not defined.
  • LCUP does not say whether an activity or assessment worded only in general terms (for example, a teaching method, or a quiz "on the topics discussed") is enough evidence. It can be read as saying too little to judge, or read together with the week's content. Choose the reading you can defend, say which one you used, and answer "yes" to "Requires an institutional decision?".

4.6 Course Content ↔ Assessment

Question. Does the assessment measure students' learning of this week's course content?

Consider

  • whether the assessment tasks name, or clearly target, the topics the week lists.
  • coverage: whether the tasks assess the main topics or only some of them.
ALIGNEDThe assessment targets the content's topics.
PARTIALLY_ALIGNEDThe assessment targets some of the topics, or targets them only in part.
NOT_ALIGNEDThe assessment targets a different subject.
INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCEThe assessment, as written, cannot be compared with the content.

Needs an institutional definition

  • LCUP does not say whether an activity or assessment worded only in general terms (for example, a teaching method, or a quiz "on the topics discussed") is enough evidence. It can be read as saying too little to judge, or read together with the week's content. Choose the reading you can defend, say which one you used, and answer "yes" to "Requires an institutional decision?".

4.7 Learning Activities ↔ Assessment

Question. Does the assessment measure what the learning activities prepare students to do?

Consider

  • the correspondence between what students practise in the activities and what the tasks ask of them.
  • whether the tasks ask for something that no activity prepares students for.
ALIGNEDThe tasks assess what the activities prepare students to do.
PARTIALLY_ALIGNEDThe tasks assess part of it, or also ask for something the activities do not prepare.
NOT_ALIGNEDThe tasks ask for something the activities do not prepare students for.
INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCEThe activities or the tasks, as written, cannot be compared.

Needs an institutional definition

  • LCUP does not say whether an activity or assessment worded only in general terms (for example, a teaching method, or a quiz "on the topics discussed") is enough evidence. It can be read as saying too little to judge, or read together with the week's content. Choose the reading you can defend, say which one you used, and answer "yes" to "Requires an institutional decision?".
  • Whether every activity must be assessed in the same week is not defined: an assessment may come in a later week, or the activity may be formative.

Part 5. Recording your judgment

Faculty judgmentOne of the four codes, exactly as listed.
Academic justificationQuote the words you relied on from both statements, in quotation marks and exactly as printed. Then explain, in one to three sentences, why they support your judgment. For ILO ↔ CLO, say how you read the CLO's classification if it mattered.
Recommended correction, if necessaryNeeded when your judgment is PARTIALLY_ALIGNED or NOT_ALIGNED; optional for INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE. Name the field to change (course content, ILO, CLO mapping, learning activity or assessment) and propose wording. The fix may belong to a field other than the two compared; say so. Do not propose changes to the institution's Vision, Mission, EGAs or Program Outcomes, and keep to what alignment needs: the institution has not yet set limits on recommended corrections. Write "None" if no correction is needed.
Reviewer IDThe code the research team gave you, not your name.
Within your expertise? (optional)Answer "no" or "uncertain" if the subject is outside your field. Your judgment is still recorded.
Requires an institutional decision? (optional)Answer "yes" if your judgment depends on a definition or policy that LCUP has not provided (Part 6).
Notes (optional)Anything else, including structural problems you noticed or text you think was transcribed wrongly.

Part 7. What not to do

  • Do not use any AI tool, chatbot or syllabus checker while reviewing, and do not paste the case text into any online service.
  • Do not discuss the cases with other reviewers or with the syllabus authors until you have returned your form.
  • Do not edit the evidence. If you think a statement was transcribed wrongly, say so under Notes.
  • Do not assume that a relationship is aligned because the syllabus was approved, or misaligned because it was chosen for review.
  • Judge each relationship on its own. A week or an ILO that appears in two cases may receive different judgments.

Course description

This course introduces students to the vital role of computers and computer programmers in shaping modern society. It aims to foster a strong foundation in logical formulation techniques essential for programming, while promoting the development of good coding practices. Students will be taught the fundamentals of Java programming and explore significant real-world applications. In line with La Consolacion University Philippines' commitment to forming Christo-centric Catholic professionals , the course integrates Marian Augustinian values in each lesson, ensuring that students not only achieve intellectual growth but also cultivate moral and ethical responsibility in the digital age. This holistic approach nurtures individuals who contribute positively to society, embodying community, humility, and love of service , as exemplified by the Blessed Virgin Mary and Saint Augustine. By the end of the course, students will have created their own Java applications, demonstrating both technical proficiency and a sense of purpose rooted in their Catholic faith.

Course description

This course introduces students to the principles and practices of systems engineering, focusing on the analysis, design, and management of complex systems. Through a blend of theoretical concepts and practical applications, students learn how to approach and solve large-scale engineering problems involving multiple components, stakeholders, and operational constraints. Key topics include requirements analysis, systems design, integration, project management, and performance evaluation. Students will develop skills in modeling and optimizing systems that integrate people, processes, and technology. They will explore methods for ensuring that systems are efficient, reliable, and adaptable across various industries, such as manufacturing, healthcare, and logistics. This course prepares BSIE students to play a vital role in creating systems that maximize performance, minimize waste, and support organizational goals. Beyond technical knowledge, the course integrates Marian Augustinian values into each lesson, fostering the development of students as Christo-centric Catholic professionals. The curriculum not only aims for intellectual growth but also instills a commitment to ethical decision-making, social responsibility, and compassionate service. Students will be equipped with the knowledge and values necessary to thrive as professionals in the field of Industrial Engineering.

CpE 304 Fundamentals of Mixed Signals and Sensors

BSCPE · 5 cases (FV-17, FV-18, FV-19, FV-20, FV-21) · units: lecture 3, laboratory 0 · prerequisite: FUNEC, FUNELC

Course description

This course covers operational amplifiers, signal converters, power switching devices and the construction and operation of sensors and transducers for converting physical parameters into electrical signals and vice-versa. The course focuses on the application of these devices in developing signal conversion circuits that allows measurement, processing and control of physical parameters by digital processing systems such as a finite state machine or a digital computer. Topics on actuators are also included. Anchored in the Marian and Augustinian values of truth (veritas), unity (unitas), and charity (caritas), the course encourages students to design technology that promotes social responsibility, collaboration, and compassion. Through hands-on projects, learners will not only develop technical proficiency but also embody ethical stewardship — using their knowledge to create solutions that serve the needs of others and contribute to the common good.
